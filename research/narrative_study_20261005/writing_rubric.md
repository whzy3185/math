# A revision rubric for proof driven graph theory papers

The checklist below is an original editorial instrument for the three manuscripts. The source observations are documented separately in the seven reading notes. Its purpose is to make the mathematics evaluable. It is not a scale for mathematical importance, originality, journal quality, or correctness.

## The central writing decision

Choose one sentence that identifies the actual mathematical task: an exact bound, an equality classification, a stability scale, a general implication, an explicit obstruction, or a uniform proof mechanism. A paper can contain several of these, but the reader must know which is the organizing result. Do not make “we study graph parameter X” carry this burden.

Before editing, create a four-column private working ledger: statement; hypotheses and quantifiers; evidence/proof location; provenance. Use it to prevent improvements in prose from changing what has been established. The ledger can be distilled into the article rather than printed in administrative form.

## Twelve checks

For each check use 0 = missing or misleading, 1 = present but the reader must reconstruct the point, 2 = explicit and appropriately placed. A zero on scope, provenance, or evidence is a blocking issue. Do not turn the sum into an acceptance or novelty score.

### 1 The mathematical question

Can an unfamiliar graph theorist say, before the first long definition list, what is being determined and why the already-known statement does not answer that particular question? A well-defined task is sufficient. Claiming that nobody has addressed it requires a separate literature basis.

### 2 The predecessor to present transition

State the nearest relevant predecessor with its correct result type and scope. Then identify the exact difference: different parameter range, equality cases, quantitative distance, altered model, or proof mechanism. A chronology of papers is not a comparison. Do not treat changing notation, a new proof, or adding computations as automatically changing the theorem.

### 3 An abstract with a readable spine

The abstract should identify the setting, state the principal conclusion, explain one reason it matters mathematically, identify the mechanism, and mark a material evidence boundary. These are jobs, not a mandatory five-sentence template. Remove subsidiary enumeration details if they obscure the theorem. Keep exceptions that change the advertised result.

### 4 Definitions at the moment of need

Define a quantity before stating a theorem about it. Defer notation used only in the proof. Explain exceptional conventions: index versus spectral radius, minimum versus minimal domination, vertex-disjoint versus potentially overlapping forbidden configurations, embedded subset orbit versus abstract isomorphism class. Do not rely on the reader guessing the intended standard.

### 5 A visible theorem hierarchy

Label the main theorem, sharp specialization, equality restriction, algorithmic census, and corollary according to their roles. Presentation order may differ from proof order, but tell the reader why. A large theorem count can hide a simple dependency chain. Conversely, combining unrelated statements into one large theorem can conceal different assumptions and evidence.

### 6 Significance stated as a mathematical comparison

Explain what changes in the question after the theorem. Useful comparisons include a different stability scale, a uniform bound independent of a construction parameter, or an exact witness matching a universal upper estimate. Avoid unsupported words such as first, breakthrough, surprising, complete, or optimal. If such a word remains, its object and justification should be adjacent.

### 7 Sharpness with a scope label

For every sharpness claim record the optimized quantity, permitted parameter regime, witness, and whether the assertion concerns an exact constant, leading term, order of growth, or method-specific ceiling. Distinguish attained values from suprema and limits. A witness showing an upper bound is not a lower bound over all objects.

### 8 A causal proof roadmap

Describe the obstacle, the reduction that removes it, the quantity preserved or controlled, and the final inference. “Section 2 gives preliminaries” is navigation. “Deleting the distinguished vertices permits projection to the base graph, leaving only small independence cases” is explanation. Do not claim the route is elementary merely because individual operations are elementary; the reader still needs the organizing idea.

### 9 Local lemmas with explicit jobs

Before a technical lemma, identify the output the next step needs. After a long calculation, restate what it has bought. Keep repeated arguments under a common lemma when the hypotheses really agree. Do not hide different energy levels, boundary conditions, or degenerate cases behind “similarly.”

### 10 Examples and figures that reduce work

A useful figure reveals construction, forbidden configuration, boundary coupling, or exceptional case. Label the objects that appear in the proof. Explain what a table certifies. A rendered graph does not prove its optimum; an exact coloring or rational certificate needs a verification rule. Avoid decorative schematics that omit the edges relevant to the argument.

### 11 Evidence and provenance in the right places

Keep inherited formula, new proof, new application, finite search, and formalized subclaim distinct. Credit an old witness even when it is recovered by a new framework. State what an independent replay checks and what a formal development actually proves. A hash identifies an artifact; it does not verify its theorem. Avoid repeating the same disclaimer so often that it replaces the mathematical discussion.

### 12 Limitations that generate a precise next question

End by identifying what the theorem does not control and what additional argument would be needed. Examples include a structural lower bound for global minimization, the boundary case of a sufficient hypothesis, or stability beyond two residual vertices. Do not inflate a weakness into an open problem of the field without checking its status. A limitation of the present proof may be stated as such.

## A practical editing sequence

1. Freeze the current mathematical statements and record file hashes
2. Write a one-paragraph account of question, result, mechanism, and boundary
3. Map each introduction paragraph to one job; merge repetitive jobs and split overloaded ones
4. Place the principal theorem and an explanatory paragraph before lengthy technical preparation
5. Add one proof-roadmap paragraph and, if useful, one accurately labeled schematic
6. Reconcile every abstract assertion with an exact theorem or verified certificate
7. Compare statement blocks and constants before and after revision
8. Compile, inspect the rendered pages, then ask an independent reader to recover the central claim without being coached

## What must not transfer from the sources

Do not copy unusual phrasing, imitate an author’s rhetorical personality, or reproduce diagrams. Do not import their priority claims, strength adjectives, or theorem names into unrelated problems. The transferable unit is an explanatory relationship: why a predecessor leaves a task, why a lemma resolves an obstacle, why a witness proves the stated kind of sharpness. These are common mathematical communication practices; the affiliation-based sample does not make them nationally exclusive.

Publication priority, theorem correctness, proof completeness, and citation coverage must remain separate review questions. A narrative revision passes only when it improves clarity while preserving those distinctions.
