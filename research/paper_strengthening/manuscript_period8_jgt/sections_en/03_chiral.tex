\section{Half-cell chiral symmetry}
\label{sec:chiral}

The finite Bloch sum reduces dimension but does not normally simplify an
individual fiber.  A further reduction becomes possible when half-period
translation reverses the step-two coefficient word.  The alternating sign
needed to reverse the step-one part then produces an anticommuting monomial
operator.  The coefficient calculation below characterizes this mechanism
within that natural class.

\subsection{The half-cell operator}

Let $p=2m$.  On the lifted sequence space define
\begin{equation}
 (Dx)_i=(-1)^i x_i,\qquad
 (T_mx)_i=x_{i+m},\qquad K_m=DT_m.
 \label{eq:half-cell-operators}
\end{equation}
The operator $D$ reverses every displacement-one coupling and preserves every
displacement-two coupling.  Translation by $m$ compares the two half-cells.
Their product is therefore the first monomial candidate for an operator that
anticommutes with $A_\tau$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:coefficient-chiral}
For a $2m$-periodic sign word $\tau$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item $K_mA_\tau=-A_\tau K_m$;
 \item $\tau_{i+m}=-\tau_i$ for every $i\in\mathbb Z$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Apply the anticommutator to an arbitrary sequence.  The displacement-one
terms cancel, and direct collection of the two remaining coordinates gives
\begin{align}
 ((A_\tau K_m+K_mA_\tau)x)_i
 =(-1)^i\bigl[&
   (\tau_{i-2}+\tau_{i+m-2})x_{i+m-2}\notag\\
  &+(\tau_i+\tau_{i+m})x_{i+m+2}\bigr].
 \label{eq:anticommutator-coefficients}
\end{align}
If the second half-word is the negative of the first, both coefficients
vanish.  Conversely, if the anticommutator vanishes on every sequence, the
coefficient of $x_{i+m+2}$ vanishes for every $i$, which gives
$\tau_{i+m}=-\tau_i$.  The other coefficient then vanishes as well.
\end{proof}

The operator $K_m$ preserves every Bloch space $\mathcal B_z^{(2m)}$, but it
is not yet an involution.  On this space,
\begin{equation}
 T_m^2=zI,\qquad T_mD=(-1)^mDT_m,\qquad
 K_m^2=(-1)^mzI.
 \label{eq:km-square}
\end{equation}
Choose a unit scalar $\gamma_m(z)$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
 \gamma_m(z)^2=(-1)^m z^{-1}.
 \label{eq:gamma-normalization}
\end{equation}
If $\xi^2=z$, one may take $\gamma_m(z)=\xi^{-1}$ for even $m$ and
$\gamma_m(z)=\mathrm i\xi^{-1}$ for odd $m$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 J_z=\gamma_m(z)K_m|_{\mathcal B_z^{(2m)}}
 \label{eq:normalized-j}
\end{equation}
is unitary and satisfies $J_z^2=I$; hence $J_z=J_z^*$.  Changing the square
root of $z$ replaces $J_z$ by $-J_z$ and only interchanges its two eigenspaces.

\subsection{The flux criterion}

The coefficient condition is transparent in $\tau$, but $Q$ is the local word
used in the extremal analysis.  The next equivalence shows that chirality is
visible without choosing either lift of $Q$.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:flux-chiral}
For a $2m$-periodic sign word $\tau$, the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
 \item $\tau_{i+m}=-\tau_i$ for every $i$;
 \item $Q_{i+m}=Q_i$ for every $i$ and
       $\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}Q_j=-1$.
\end{enumerate}
Under these conditions the operators $J_z$ in
\eqref{eq:normalized-j} are chiral involutions:
\begin{equation}
 J_zH_\tau^{(2m)}(z)=-H_\tau^{(2m)}(z)J_z
 \qquad(|z|=1).
 \label{eq:fiber-anticommutation}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If $\tau_{i+m}=-\tau_i$, then
\[
 Q_{i+m}=(-\tau_i)(-\tau_{i+1})=Q_i,
 \qquad
 \prod_{j=0}^{m-1}Q_j=\tau_0\tau_m=-1.
\]
For the converse, put $r_i=\tau_{i+m}/\tau_i\in\{\pm1\}$.  Half-periodicity
of $Q$ gives $r_ir_{i+1}=1$, so all $r_i$ equal one constant $r$.  Telescoping
over a half-cell gives
\[
 r=\frac{\tau_m}{\tau_0}=\prod_{j=0}^{m-1}Q_j=-1.
\]
Thus $\tau_{i+m}=-\tau_i$.  The anticommutation follows from
Theorem~\ref{thm:coefficient-chiral}; scalar normalization does not change it.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Algebraic consequences}

The monomial matrix of $J_z$ has no fixed coordinate, so its trace is zero.
As an involution on a $2m$-dimensional space, its $+1$ and $-1$ eigenspaces
therefore both have dimension $m$.  Equation
\eqref{eq:fiber-anticommutation} sends either eigenspace to the other.  In a
unitary basis adapted to this decomposition,
\begin{equation}
 H_\tau^{(2m)}(z)\simeq
 \begin{pmatrix}0&B(z)^*\\ B(z)&0\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 H_\tau^{(2m)}(z)^2\simeq
 \begin{pmatrix}B(z)^*B(z)&0\\0&B(z)B(z)^*\end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:general-chiral-block}
\end{equation}
Consequently the spectrum is symmetric about zero and
\begin{equation}
 \det(\lambda I_{2m}-H_\tau^{(2m)}(z))
 =\det(\lambda^2I_m-B(z)^*B(z)).
 \label{eq:general-even-characteristic}
\end{equation}

The two formulations describe the same phenomenon at different levels.
Equation~\eqref{eq:anticommutator-coefficients} is an operator statement: the
alternating diagonal sign reverses displacement-one couplings and half-cell
translation reverses the displacement-two coefficients.  Theorem
\ref{thm:flux-chiral} makes the criterion intrinsic to the signed graph:
chirality can be read from the switching-invariant local cycle word $Q$ and
its half-cell product.

This symmetry halves the spectral dimension but does not make a general
$m\times m$ block explicitly solvable.  Nor does it force an edge below $8$:
the alternating period-two word satisfies the criterion and has edge exactly
$8$.  The period-eight word \eqref{eq:target-words} is different in two ways.
It is the first strict crossing of the reference edge, and its $4\times4$
squared block has a second algebraic symmetry.  That additional structure is
what makes the complete dispersion accessible.
