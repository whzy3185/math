\section{Introduction and main results}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{From switching to fixed-graph spectral optimization}

A signed graph consists of an underlying graph and a choice of sign on each
edge.  Its adjacency matrix has the same zero pattern as the ordinary
adjacency matrix, but its nonzero entries may be $1$ or $-1$.  This modest
change separates two kinds of information.  Individual edge signs depend on
the chosen representative: switching at vertices conjugates the adjacency
matrix by a diagonal sign matrix and therefore leaves its spectrum unchanged.
Products of signs around cycles, by contrast, survive switching.  Balance,
switching classes, and cycle signs have consequently been central coordinates
in signed graph theory since the work of Harary and Zaslavsky
\cite{Harary1953,Zaslavsky1982}.

The spectral question has two distinct forms.  One may ask whether a graph
admits a signing with eigenvalues controlled by a universal benchmark.  This
viewpoint is fundamental in the theory of two-lifts: Bilu and Linial connected
signings with new eigenvalues of lifts, and the interlacing-polynomial method
of Marcus, Spielman, and Srivastava produced Ramanujan signings in the
bipartite setting \cite{BiluLinial2006,MarcusSpielmanSrivastava2015}.  A finer
problem fixes the entire underlying graph and asks for the least spectral
radius among all its signings,
\begin{equation}
 m(G)=\min_{\sigma:E(G)\to\{\pm1\}}\rho(A_\sigma).
 \label{eq:fixed-minimum}
\end{equation}
Here the support, order, and degree sequence are held constant; the remaining
optimization takes place among switching-invariant cycle patterns.  This
problem appears naturally in the broader signed spectral programme
\cite{BelardoEtAl2018}, and exact extremal results are known for several
restricted signed graph families \cite{BrunettiStanic2022,GhorbaniMajidi2024}.
The fixed-graph version is nevertheless rigid: an existence theorem for a good
signing does not identify the minimum, and a highly symmetric candidate need
not be optimal.

\subsection{Why cycle squares and periodic signings}

We study \eqref{eq:fixed-minimum} for
\[
 C_n(1,2),\qquad
 V=\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z,\qquad
 i\sim j\iff i-j\equiv\pm1,\pm2\pmod n.
\]
This graph is the square of the $n$-cycle when $n$ is in the range considered
below.  It occupies a useful middle ground.  The unsigned graph is circulant,
so its spectrum is read from scalar Fourier symbols \cite{Davis1979}; recent
signed spectral work on nonbipartite Cayley graphs provides a broader
neighboring setting \cite{HuLiu2025}.  At the same time, every pair of consecutive step-one edges
and the corresponding step-two edge forms a triangle.  These overlapping
triangles make the cycle data local and strongly coupled: changing one edge
alters several nearby cycles.

An arbitrary signing destroys one-site translation invariance.  A periodic
cycle-sign pattern restores translation by a cell, replacing a scalar Fourier
symbol with a small Hermitian matrix-valued symbol.  This is the finite graph
counterpart of the Bloch decomposition for periodic discrete operators.
Gauge, flux, and quasi-momentum play the same distinct roles in unit-gain and
periodic magnetic graph theory \cite{Reff2012,KorotyaevSaburova2017,
KorotyaevSaburova2023}.  In the present finite setting no direct-integral
machinery is required: a ring consisting of $L$ cells is an orthogonal direct
sum of $L$ finite fibers.

These observations point to the correct variables.  After switching the
step-one Hamilton cycle to a local positive gauge, let $\tau_i$ denote the sign
of the triangle based at $i$, and let $\alpha\in\{\pm1\}$ retain the global
Hamilton-cycle holonomy.  The finite boundary condition is quasi-periodic,
$x_{i+n}=\alpha x_i$.  For local square calculations it is convenient to pass
once more to
\[
 Q_i=\tau_i\tau_{i+1}.
\]
The word $Q$ records where odd-displacement couplings survive after the
operator is squared.  Thus periodicity is not imposed to make an enumeration
smaller; it is the setting in which local flux, cell translation, and the
spectral edge can be compared exactly.

The phase central to this paper has primitive period eight,
\begin{equation}
 \tau_*=(1,1,-1,1,-1,-1,1,-1),\qquad
 Q_*=(1,-1,-1,-1,1,-1,-1,-1).
 \label{eq:target-words}
\end{equation}
The two positive entries of $Q_*$ are antipodal.  More importantly, the second
half of $\tau_*$ is the negative of the first.  That half-period relation is
the source of an anticommuting symmetry, not an accidental property of an
$8\times8$ determinant.  Figure~\ref{fig:period8-cell} displays the cell and
the two levels of flux data.

\subsection{Main results}

Let $A_{8L,+}^{*}$ be the finite signed adjacency matrix obtained by repeating
\eqref{eq:target-words} over $L$ cells with positive Hamilton holonomy.  Set
\begin{equation}
 \etaedge=4+\sqrt{10+2\sqrt5}.
 \label{eq:eta}
\end{equation}
Our first result gives an exact finite value rather than an asymptotic band
edge or a numerical upper bound.

\begin{theorem}[Exact finite period-eight phase]
\label{thm:main-exact}
For every integer $L\geq1$,
\[
 \rho(A_{8L,+}^{*})^2=\etaedge.
\]
If $A_{8L}^{\mathrm{tw}}$ denotes the twisted signing defined in
\cref{sec:twisted}, then for every $L\geq4$,
\[
 \rho(A_{8L,+}^{*})^2=\etaedge
   <\rho(A_{8L}^{\mathrm{tw}})^2.
\]
Consequently $m(C_{8L}(1,2))<\rho(A_{8L}^{\mathrm{tw}})$ for every $L\geq4$.
\end{theorem}

The exact solution comes from a structural statement valid at every even
period.  It identifies precisely when the most natural signed half-cell
translation is a chiral symmetry.  The term \emph{chiral} is used here in its
linear-algebraic sense: a self-adjoint involution anticommutes with the
Hermitian fiber.  Related spectral-symmetry mechanisms occur for signed graph
Laplacians and chiral Floquet systems
\cite{AtayHua2016,CedzichEtAl2021}; the criterion below concerns the present
signed adjacency operator and its local flux word.

\begin{theorem}[Half-cell flux criterion]
\label{thm:main-chiral}
Let $p=2m$ and let $\tau$ be a $2m$-periodic Hamilton-gauge word.  Within the
monomial class consisting of the alternating diagonal sign followed by
half-period translation, a normalized chiral involution exists on every Bloch
fiber if and only if
\[
 \tau_{i+m}=-\tau_i\qquad(i\in\mathbb Z).
\]
Equivalently,
\[
 Q_{i+m}=Q_i\quad(i\in\mathbb Z),\qquad
 \prod_{j=0}^{m-1}Q_j=-1.
\]
Whenever these conditions hold, the characteristic polynomial is even and
the squared fiber problem reduces from dimension $2m$ to dimension $m$.
\end{theorem}

The general criterion explains why the period-eight fiber reduces, but does
not by itself explain why period eight is selected by the spectral threshold
$8$.  The next theorem supplies that missing extremal statement.  Period is
understood primitively; repeating a shorter cell does not count as a new
phase.

\begin{theorem}[First occurrence and rigidity]
\label{thm:main-rigidity}
Eight is the smallest primitive period of a legal Hamilton-gauge signing whose
squared Bloch edge is strictly below $8$.  At primitive period eight, the word
$Q_*$ in \eqref{eq:target-words} is the unique sub-eight class, up to cyclic
translation, reflection, and the two lifts $\tau$ and $-\tau$.  The balanced
class has squared edge $8$, and every other legal period-eight class has edge
strictly above $8$.
\end{theorem}

The first three phase-averaged even moments also give a period-independent
constraint.  If $d$ is the number of positive $Q$-sites and $a,b$ count
positive pairs at cyclic distances one and two, respectively, then an edge at
most $8$ forces
\begin{equation}
 d\leq\frac{3p}{4},\qquad
 40d+96a+48b\leq42p.
 \label{eq:intro-moment-obstruction}
\end{equation}
This does not classify longer words; it identifies the sparse, weakly
clustered defect geometry behind the short-period result.

\subsection{Position and proof architecture}

Theorem~\ref{thm:main-exact} also resolves a specific recent proposal.  The
twisted signing was put forward as an optimal candidate for this family in
\cite{Suvagiya2026}.  Our formulation does not rely on that proposal: the
fixed-graph optimization problem, the period-eight phase, and the chiral
criterion are defined independently.  The recent conjecture is one consequence
of the strict comparison, rather than the source of the problem.

The proof follows the mathematical dependencies.  Section~\ref{sec:gauge}
passes from edge signs to $(\tau,\alpha)$, proves the symmetry conventions, and
derives the finite Bloch sum.  Section~\ref{sec:chiral} proves
Theorem~\ref{thm:main-chiral} by a coefficient calculation and a flux
telescoping identity.  Section~\ref{sec:period8} specializes the reduction,
solves the period-eight polynomial, quantizes both finite holonomy sectors, and
proves Theorem~\ref{thm:main-exact}.  Section~\ref{sec:first-rigidity} asks
whether the appearance of period eight is accidental; moment inequalities and
exact finite certificates prove Theorem~\ref{thm:main-rigidity}.
Section~\ref{sec:defect-obstruction} extracts the general defect obstruction
\eqref{eq:intro-moment-obstruction}, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} closes with the remaining exact minimization
problem.
